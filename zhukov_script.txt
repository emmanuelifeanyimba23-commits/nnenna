On the first of June, 1946, the most decorated soldier in the Soviet Union walked into a meeting room in Moscow and found himself on trial. No judge. No jury. Just a long table of marshals and generals, and near the head of it, a short man in a plain grey tunic, smoking a pipe.

The charges were read aloud from a dossier prepared by the secret police. The accused, it said, was arrogant. Disloyal. He had taken credit for victories that belonged to others. He had gathered a circle of officers who looked to him, and not to the Party. One of those officers, the marshal in charge of the air force, had already been arrested, beaten, and kept awake for days until he signed a statement against his friend.

The word that hung over the room was one every Soviet officer dreaded. Bonapartism. The suspicion that a general had grown popular enough to become a Napoleon.

Here is what makes that scene so strange. Less than a year earlier, this same man had ridden a white horse across Red Square to lead the Victory Parade, while the man with the pipe watched from the top of Lenin's tomb. Barely a month before that parade, he had accepted the surrender of Nazi Germany in a Berlin suburb. He was the general who had held Moscow, trapped an entire German army at Stalingrad, and taken Berlin.

And here is the part that should stop you cold. None of that saved him. It was the reason he was in the room.

Within days he was stripped of command of the Soviet ground forces and sent south to run the Odessa military district, a posting so far from power it was practically an insult with a uniform attached. Two years later, they pushed him further still, to the Urals. Agents searched his homes and listed every painting and every roll of cloth he had brought back from Germany, building a case that could have ended with a bullet.

He had survived Hitler's army. Now he had to survive his own government.

Born to peasants. Apprenticed to a furrier at eleven. A cavalry sergeant in the Tsar's army. The marshal who saved the Soviet Union, and the one man in it Stalin could never bring himself to trust.

His name was Georgy Zhukov. So how does a furrier's boy become the general a dictator fears? It comes down to three decisions. Let's break down Georgy Zhukov's three greatest tactical moves of the Second World War.

Before the battles, one detail explains almost everything that follows. Zhukov was not trained to be cautious. He was trained to be hard. As a young cavalryman in the First World War, he won two St. George Crosses, one of them for capturing a German officer, and he was left half deaf by a mine blast. When the Revolution came, he joined the Red cavalry and spent the next twenty years rising through the hardest school the new army had. He was blunt. He was stubborn. Subordinates feared his temper, and superiors found that he would argue with almost anyone. But in the 1930s, he also absorbed a revolutionary Soviet idea called deep operations. Do not simply push the enemy back. Break through, swing around, and destroy him from behind. He was waiting for a chance to try it for real. He was about to get one, at the far edge of the world.

The first one happened in a war most people have never heard of.

Summer, 1939. Eastern Mongolia. An empty sea of grass on the border between Soviet-backed Mongolia and the Japanese puppet state of Manchukuo. Through it ran a slow river called the Khalkhin Gol. The Japanese army in the region, the Kwantung Army, insisted the border sat on the river. The Mongolians said it sat about 20 km further east. In May, the shooting started over that strip of grass, and by June, it was a real war that neither Moscow nor Tokyo had ever officially declared.

Zhukov arrived in early June. He was 42, a corps commander, and he carried a quiet fear with him. In the previous two years, Stalin's purges had shot or imprisoned thousands of Red Army officers, including three of the five marshals. Zhukov had watched friends vanish overnight. Denunciations had already been written against him. Mongolia was a test, and he knew exactly what happened to men who failed tests.

The Japanese made one deeply comfortable assumption. They believed the Soviets simply could not fight a big battle out there. The nearest Soviet railhead was roughly 650 km away across open steppe. No proper roads. Almost no water. Every shell, every litre of fuel, every loaf of bread had to come by truck, and a single round trip could take five days. Any large Soviet offensive, the Japanese reasoned, would starve before it started. And if the Soviets did build up, they would see it coming, because you cannot hide an army on a billiard table.

Zhukov looked at the same flat, empty plain and saw a stage. If the Japanese could see everything, then he would decide exactly what they saw.

First, the trucks. He gathered a fleet of more than 4,000 lorries and ran them around the clock, mostly at night, until roughly 57,000 men and close to 500 tanks were in position.

Then, the performance. His radio operators sent messages in codes the Japanese could break, all of them about digging in for winter. Requests for stoves. Requests for building timber. He had a pamphlet printed called What the Soviet Soldier Must Know in Defence, and made sure copies drifted into Japanese hands. Loudspeakers played the sound of pile drivers, night after night, as if the whole army were hammering in fence posts. And for nearly two weeks, tanks drove up and down the line in the dark with their mufflers stripped off, roaring for hours and then going nowhere at all.

Think about what that did. By August, the Japanese had stopped listening. The roar of engines at night had become as ordinary as the wind.

At dawn on August 20th, a Sunday, the roar was real. Artillery and bombers hit the Japanese centre while armoured columns swung wide around both of its flanks. The centre was a fist to pin the enemy in place. The wings were the trap. Within days the two pincers met behind the Japanese 23rd Division. By the end of the month, the pocket had been wiped out.

Now, let us be honest with the numbers, because this victory was not cheap. Japanese records count roughly 8,000 dead. Soviet archives, opened decades later, admit to around 9,700 killed or missing on their own side. Zhukov paid more lives than he took. That would become a pattern.

But measure it by what it bought. Tokyo was shaken to its core. Japan's generals quietly gave up on a war northward into Siberia, and in April 1941, Japan signed a neutrality pact with the Soviet Union. Their ambitions turned south instead, toward the Pacific, and toward a harbour in Hawaii.

And that meant something else. The great Soviet armies in the Far East, sitting on guard against Japan, could one day be moved. Remember those divisions. Two years later, they would arrive at the gates of Moscow.

The lesson of Khalkhin Gol is simple. If the enemy can see everything, give him something to look at. Train his ears until your attack sounds like nothing at all.

October 1941. Germany had invaded the Soviet Union four months earlier, and the result had been one of the greatest military collapses in modern history. Now Hitler launched Operation Typhoon, the final drive on Moscow. In its first weeks, near the towns of Vyazma and Bryansk, entire Soviet armies were encircled. Germany claimed more than 600,000 prisoners. The road to the capital stood almost open.

On October 10th, Stalin handed what was left of the Western Front to Zhukov. In Moscow, government offices were evacuated east. Crowds looted shops. Factories were wired with explosives, ready to be blown.

By late November, German forward units were roughly 30 km from the Kremlin. Some accounts claim German officers could make out the city through their field glasses.

And the German high command was sure of one thing. The Red Army was spent. Their intelligence officers had counted every Soviet division destroyed since June and concluded there could not be much left. The Russians were bleeding out. One more push, and the city would fall before the snow got deep.

Zhukov's insight was the most dangerous kind, because it demanded that he do almost nothing with his best troops. He understood that Moscow would not be saved by throwing every fresh division into the line the moment it arrived. That would only feed the German machine one meal at a time. Moscow would be saved by holding with the bare minimum, letting the Germans exhaust themselves, and keeping a hammer hidden behind the city until the attackers had nothing left.

Picture what that meant on the ground. Ragged rifle divisions, and militia units of factory workers and students, holding the line with whatever they had, while fresh armies sat waiting to the rear. Some of those fresh troops were the divisions from Siberia and the Far East. A Soviet spy in Tokyo, Richard Sorge, had reported that Japan would not attack in the east. Khalkhin Gol had already taught Japan that lesson, and now the dividend was paid.

At one point that November, Stalin called Zhukov and asked him a question that could have been a trap. Are you sure we will hold Moscow? Answer me honestly, as a communist. Zhukov did not flatter him. He said they would hold it, but he needed two more armies and at least 200 tanks. He got the armies. The tanks were another matter.

Then the weather turned. Temperatures fell far below minus 30. German engines would not start. Rifles froze. Men in summer uniforms lost fingers and toes. Typhoon blew itself out on the frozen edges of the city.

On December 5th and 6th, 1941, the hammer came down. Soviet armies, many of them never spotted by German intelligence, struck along a front hundreds of kilometres long. White-clad ski troops. Cavalry moving through snowbound forests. Tanks with wide tracks that rode over the drifts. The German army had never been hit like this.

If you watched our episode on Heinz Guderian, this is the other side of his story. This is the counterattack that sent his frozen panzers reeling and cost him his command.

Within weeks, the Germans were driven back between 100 and 250 km. Hitler sacked the commander in chief of his army and took the job himself. For the first time since the war began, the German army had suffered a strategic defeat on land.

Here is the honest ledger. The Soviet counteroffensive overreached through the winter, and the cost in lives was terrible. But put a clock on what mattered. Two months earlier, the Soviet state had been packing its bags. Now the German army was retreating through the snow, and the myth that it could not be beaten had cracked for good.

The lesson of Moscow cuts against every instinct. The hardest discipline in war is holding your reserve while everything around you is burning. Spend it too early, and you only feed the fire. Spend it at the right moment, and you put the fire out.

But Moscow was a defence that turned into an attack. The third move would be something else entirely. A trap built so carefully that the enemy walked into it on their own.

September 1942. The war had reached the Volga. The German 6th Army, under General Friedrich Paulus, was fighting its way into the city of Stalingrad street by street, house by house, sometimes room by room. The Soviet defenders under General Vasily Chuikov clung to a strip of riverbank, in places only a few hundred metres deep.

On September 13th, Zhukov and the Red Army's chief of staff, Aleksandr Vasilevsky, stood in Stalin's office. While Stalin studied a map, the two generals murmured to each other that they needed, as they put it, another solution. Stalin overheard. He asked what solution they meant. Then he sent them away to work it out.

The German assumption was this. The Soviets were pouring everything they had into the city, and nothing would be left for anything else. So the Germans, too, fed their best units into the ruins, and left the long, open flanks north and south of Stalingrad to their allies. Romanian armies. Brave men, but thinly spread, short of anti-tank guns, and with almost no armour of their own.

Zhukov and Vasilevsky looked at the burning city and saw bait. Stalingrad did not need to win. It only needed to hold, just barely, and keep the 6th Army's attention locked inside the rubble. Chuikov would get only enough men to survive. Everything else would go somewhere else.

And it went there in total silence. Over two months, more than a million Soviet troops, nearly 900 tanks, and some 13,000 guns were moved into position on the flanks. They moved by night. Radio traffic was cut to almost nothing. Commanders gave orders in person instead of by signal. Civilians in front-line villages were moved out so no one could talk. Inside the city, Chuikov's men fought on, starved of reinforcements, because the starving was part of the plan.

In the early morning of November 19th, in freezing fog, thousands of Soviet guns opened fire on the Romanian line north of the city. The next day, a second blow fell to the south. The Romanian defences crumbled. Soviet tanks poured through, not into the city, but around it, racing toward each other across the snow.

On November 23rd, the two pincers met near the town of Kalach on the Don. The ring was closed.

Now let that sink in. The German army had spent three months fighting its way to the Volga. Zhukov's trap needed four days to close behind it. Inside the ring were somewhere between 250,000 and 300,000 Axis soldiers. Hitler forbade them to break out. A relief attack in December failed. The airlift promised by the Luftwaffe delivered only a fraction of what the trapped army needed to stay alive.

On January 31st, 1943, Paulus surrendered. Two days later, the last German troops in the north of the city gave up. Around 91,000 men marched into captivity. Only about 5,000 of them would ever see Germany again.

The lesson of Stalingrad is the coldest of the three. Do not try to win the battle your enemy has chosen. Let him keep fighting it, and win the one behind his back.

After Stalingrad came Kursk, then the great summer offensives, then the long road west. In April 1945, Zhukov's armies stormed the Seelow Heights east of Berlin and fought their way into the city. On the night of May 8th, in the suburb of Karlshorst, he sat at the head of the table as German commanders signed the final surrender.

And in June, he rode that white horse across Red Square. According to a story told years later, and never proven, Stalin had meant to lead the parade himself, until the horse threw him in rehearsal.

Which brings us back to the room where we began. A victorious general. A famous name. Officers loyal to him. Foreign leaders who admired him. Eisenhower, who met him in Berlin, became something close to a friend and invited him to visit America. Stalin made sure he never went. In most countries, a man like that becomes a national treasure. To Stalin, he was a threat.

So the marshal went to Odessa. Then to the Urals. And he waited, knowing exactly what had happened to men like him in 1937.

Stalin died in 1953. Zhukov came back. He helped arrest Stalin's secret police chief, Lavrentiy Beria. He became minister of defence. In 1957, when hardliners tried to remove Nikita Khrushchev, Zhukov's support helped keep Khrushchev in power. And a few months later, Khrushchev fired him too, with the very same accusation. Bonapartism.

Two leaders. Two dismissals. One fear.

So let's lay the three moves side by side. At Khalkhin Gol, he trained his enemy to stop listening, then surrounded a division on a plain where nothing could hide. At Moscow, he held his hammer back while the city burned, and struck only when the enemy had nothing left. At Stalingrad, he turned a ruined city into bait and closed a ring around an army in four days.

Three moves, one idea. Make the enemy look at the wrong thing.

But a serious history has to show both sides of the ledger. Zhukov spent lives with a cold hand. The same autumn as Stalingrad, he led another offensive near Rzhev, codenamed Mars, and it was a bloody failure that Soviet histories buried for decades. At the Seelow Heights, his rush for Berlin cost tens of thousands of Soviet lives in a matter of days. He threatened his own officers with execution, and sometimes carried it out. His memoirs, like most generals' memoirs, made him look better than the record does. And Vasilevsky deserves far more credit for Stalingrad than the Zhukov legend usually allows.

He was not a gentle man. But he was the one Soviet commander who stood in every great crisis, from Mongolia to Berlin, and never broke.

There is a reason Russians compare him to Mikhail Kutuzov, the old field marshal who faced Napoleon in 1812. Kutuzov also defended Moscow by refusing to spend his army too early. He also waited for the winter to do its work. And his emperor also disliked him, trusting him only because the country gave him no other choice.

Zhukov died in 1974. His ashes were placed in the Kremlin Wall, at the heart of the power that had spent years keeping him at arm's length.

And that is the irony of Georgy Zhukov. Stalin feared he would reach for power. Khrushchev feared the same. Yet in all those years, with armies that would have followed him anywhere, he never once tried to seize it. The general the dictators feared most was the one who kept saving them.
